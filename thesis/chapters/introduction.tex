\chapter{Introduction}

Understanding whether one event leads to another is central to many real-world tasks, from medical diagnosis~\cite{richens:2020} to analyzing the effects of policies~\cite{blackwell:2013} or environmental changes~\cite{guevara:2025}. A particularly common formulation is a binary causal question asking whether one concept $A$ causes another concept $B$. When causal knowledge is represented as a directed graph, this problem reduces to determining whether a directed path exists between the graph nodes corresponding to $A$ and $B$. Large-scale causal knowledge graphs such as CauseNet~\cite{heindorf:2020} contain millions of cause--effect relations extracted from text, enabling causal reasoning via graph traversal. A straightforward approach is exhaustive search using Breadth-First Search (BFS), which guarantees completeness and shortest paths in terms of hop count~\cite{cormen:2022}. However, in graphs with high branching factors, BFS quickly becomes computationally expensive, often requiring the exploration of thousands of nodes before reaching a target.
Furthermore, shortest paths are not necessarily the most semantically meaningful. Due to the noisy, automatically extracted nature of large knowledge graphs, short paths may include weak or spurious causal links, limiting their usefulness as explanations. To reduce the search space, reinforcement learning (RL) traversal methods have been proposed~\cite{bluebaum:2024}. These approaches learn a policy that guides exploration toward promising regions of the graph and can significantly reduce the number of visited nodes. However, they require a dedicated agent, introduce architectural complexity, and perform repeated policy evaluations during traversal.

This thesis pursues a different direction. Instead of learning a traversal policy, the proposed approach learns a search heuristic. It employs an $A^*$ search procedure, where the heuristic is defined as the embedding distance between the current node and the target node. A pretrained sentence transformer is adapted so that embedding distances guide the search toward nodes that are se\-man\-ti\-cal\-ly closer to the target, providing an initial heuristic for reducing unnecessary exploration. Although this semantic structure is useful, semantic similarity does not necessarily correspond to causal relevance. The embedding space is therefore reshaped through fine-tuning to improve the heuristic for causal graph traversal. Training examples are derived from successful causal search trajectories, teaching the model to favor neighboring concepts that provide a shorter embedding-space route toward the target over alternative successors. Empirical evaluation shows that the learned heu\-ris\-tic substantially reduces node expansions, although this efficiency comes with a trade-off in effectiveness. Fine-tuning mainly improves search efficiency rather than F\textsubscript{1}, and uncapped BFS remains more effective in some settings due to its exhaustive exploration. Compared with the RL method of \citet{bluebaum:2024}, the proposed approach achieves higher F\textsubscript{1} and lower measured runtime in all final test settings.

The contributions of this thesis are as follows: (1) an embedding-guided \(A^*\) approach is proposed for binary causal question answering over large-scale causal knowledge graphs; (2) a search-oriented ranking objective is introduced that reshapes the embedding space using successful causal search trajectories, together with Matryoshka Representation Learning to support efficient low-dimensional heuristics; and (3) a systematic evaluation across multiple causal graphs and datasets compares the approach with BFS and RL traversal, demonstrating substantial reductions in search effort and runtime while characterizing the resulting effectiveness--efficiency trade-off. The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:foundations} introduces the theoretical foundations required for the proposed approach. Chapter~\ref{chap:related_work} reviews related work on causal question answering, causal knowledge graphs, and graph traversal methods, including RL-based approaches and learned search heuristics. Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} presents the problem formulation and the proposed embedding-guided \(A^*\) approach for causal graph traversal. Chapter~\ref{chap:train} describes the training procedure, including data construction, fine-tuning, validation, and model selection. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents the experimental evaluation and compares the proposed approach with BFS and RL across different datasets and causal graphs. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses the main findings, trade-offs, and limitations. Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the thesis and outlines directions for future work.
